\chapter{Summary and Outlook}

\section{Summary} 
\label{sec:summary_findings} 

This thesis addresses the absence of a systematic approach for verifying the security functions of industrial gateways at the physical \gls{acr:IT}/\gls{acr:OT} interface. With Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 making cybersecurity a mandatory part of the conformity assessment of machinery, established functional and safety validation processes lacked a requirement-driven method for verifying security functions. To bridge this gap, a prototype methodology was developed using the Design Science Research Methodology (\gls{acr:DSRM}). The objective was not to certify a product, but to develop and validate a systematic methodology for verifying derived component capabilities. The methodology derives required tests along a traceable chain, mapping machine-level obligations through the draft standard prEN 50742 to the component requirements of \gls{acr:IEC} 62443-4-2. Each requirement is linked to the affected assets, a \gls{acr:STRIDE} threat, and the relevant security function, with test cases specified on both a product-neutral scenario level and a concrete execution level. The approach also reuses the existing company test framework through a four-outcome tool-to-target applicability assessment and classifies each test using the verification taxonomy of \gls{acr:IEC} 62443-4-1 Practice \gls{acr:SVV}.

The central steps of the methodology were demonstrated end-to-end on a real Safe Remote I/O device in an isolated laboratory under the direct-access network position \gls{acr:NV1}. The derivation yielded fourteen derived security requirements and decomposed the device into nine security-relevant assets, establishing a traceable derivation chain that answers the first research question. Answering the second research question, the applicability assessment classified the ten company tests before execution: five were applicable and executed (Nmap, Nikto, \gls{acr:OWASP} ZAP, Siege, boofuzz), three were not applicable to the device or its flat topology (Nmap's zone-based procedure, Hydra, Lynis), one was blocked in the laboratory (OpenVAS/GVM), and one original procedure was not applicable while the tool itself was reused to produce a transport-level observation (SSLscan). The assessment therefore distinguished interfaces known to be absent in advance from interfaces whose absence was demonstrated by tool output. During execution, a set of evidence-backed test activities characterised the reachable non-safety attack surface, the identification interface, the intervention log, and application-layer availability and robustness. Two requirements, RQ-003 and RQ-012, were assessed as \textbf{Fail}: no dedicated tracing log was exposed through the examined interface, and the substitute error log carried only a relative system tick and no actor identity, and its content was cleared by a cold restart. The remaining twelve requirements were assessed as \textbf{Not Assessed}, primarily because their complete acceptance condition depends on the passive-observation variant \gls{acr:NV2}, the in-path variant \gls{acr:NV3}, an induced software or configuration change, or the engineering tool, none of which were realised in this campaign. Additionally, a structured fuzzing run revealed a temporary, self-recovering disruption of the HTTP service, together with an unstored device fault indication; although its causation was not established. While the NV1 setup provided substantial evidence and answered the third research question in part, properties depending on cyclic safety communication require passive-observation (\gls{acr:NV2}) or in-path (\gls{acr:NV3}) variants.

Ultimately, the thesis contributes a requirement-derivation model translating machine-level obligations into derived component-level specifications, a traceability chain linking requirements to concrete evidence, a four-outcome tool-to-target applicability assessment, and a network-access-variant model clarifying test feasibility. A central methodological insight of this work is the necessary distinction between functional safety reactions and cybersecurity compliance: successfully transitioning to a safe state following a fault does not inherently validate security-specific attributes such as accountability, integrity, or traceability, thereby necessitating independent verification. This distinction avoids conflating functional safety with cybersecurity conformity. Measured against the objective set out in the introduction, the results contribute to closing the identified gap: the methodology provides a traceable, evidence-based path from machine-level obligations to component-level verdicts, and the three research questions are answered within the bounded scope of the NV1 demonstration, even where individual verdicts remain Not Assessed rather than Pass or Fail. The predominance of Not Assessed verdicts is therefore a consequence of the deliberately bounded practical scope, not a failure of the methodology itself; a complete conformity assessment of the device was never the objective. For the industrial partner, the methodology is intended to support the process between regulatory expectations and functional testing by providing a reusable schema. Sustainability is treated here as a criterion spanning economic, ecological, and social dimensions. Economically, the systematic verification may reduce additional tooling effort and is intended to support earlier identification of weaknesses, which is typically less costly to remediate before a product reaches the field than after a security-related recall or an incident response. Ecologically, a reusable, requirement-driven test schema reduces the need for repeated ad-hoc test setups and bespoke tooling across product variants, and earlier detection of security weaknesses can avoid unnecessary field replacements or hardware recalls, which together lowers the material and energy footprint of the verification process. \section{Future Work} 
\label{sec:future_work}

To advance the current findings, the most immediate step is to realise the two remaining network access variants. Implementing the passive-observation variant \gls{acr:NV2} using a mirroring switch or a network tap, and the in-path variant \gls{acr:NV3} using a transparent bridge, would allow the execution of scenarios involving safety-telegram integrity, in-path manipulation, watchdog-passivation, and domain-separation. This is crucial for assessing requirements that depend on cyclic safety communication, in particular RQ-002 and RQ-005. Concurrently, outstanding activities requiring no additional variant should be completed within the same campaign, including the induced software-installation event for RQ-008, the configuration change for RQ-009, the \gls{acr:TIA} Portal-based identification and maintenance read operations for RQ-004, RQ-006, RQ-007, and RQ-013, the connection-hold procedure against the remaining identification endpoints, direct confirmation of the web-scanner Actuator finding, examination of the SNMP service, and reproduction of the fuzzing-induced HTTP disruption to establish causation. Together, these steps would extend the current partial coverage. Furthermore, applying the methodology to a device that exposes an authenticated and encrypted interface, such as over \gls{acr:HTTPS}, would additionally enable credential, transport-security, and access-control tests that were not applicable to the current device.

Beyond these immediate testing goals, the methodology itself can be extended. Because prEN 50742 grades the required test depth through the Safety-Related Security Level (\gls{acr:SRSL}), the test catalogue can be expanded so that test strictness scales with the level of the affected asset and requirement—ranging, for example, from a simple checksum check at a low level to cryptographic verification under active manipulation at a high level. Another promising direction is the integration of machine learning. A profiling module that learns the normal network behaviour of a device in the testbed could significantly support the detection of anomalous behaviour during a test \cite[pp. 12]{siboni2016securitytestbedinternetthings}.

Finally, to ensure long-term value, the methodology should be permanently embedded in the development and quality-management processes. This includes assigning the responsibilities of the governance model described in Appendix \ref{app:SecureDevelopmentLifecycle}, fixing the point in the lifecycle at which tests are generated and evaluated, and integrating execution into a continuous integration and delivery pipeline for automated security regression testing. It also requires maintaining the traceability matrix when the normative basis changes, such as when prEN 50742 is published in its final form. The allocation of evidentiary duties with the machine manufacturer should be confirmed when the standard reaches its final form.
