\chapter{Implementation, Result and Discussion} 
\label{chap:implementation_results} 

This chapter applies the methodology developed in  Chapter \ref{chap:methodology} to a physical \gls{acr:SUT}. It documents the implemented environment, the instantiation of the method, the collected evidence, the verification of the derived requirements, the findings, and the reliability of the results.

The practical work covers the direct-access variant \gls{acr:NV1} defined in Section \ref{subsec:nv1}. The passive-observation variant \gls{acr:NV2} and the in-path variant \gls{acr:NV3} are part of the methodology but were not realised in the laboratory. The validation therefore covers the core \gls{acr:NV1} process and those requirements whose acceptance conditions are observable from the direct-access position. The tests that depend on \gls{acr:NV2} or \gls{acr:NV3} remain specified but not executed.



\section{Scope of the Practical Demonstration}
\label{sec:testbed_setup} 

This section defines the object, the environment, and the boundaries of the practical demonstration.

\subsection{System Under Test} 
\label{subsec:test_object} 

The \gls{acr:SUT} is the \gls{acr:SRIO}, a fieldbus-based safety component that exchanges safety-related input and output data with a higher-level safety controller over \gls{acr:PROFINET} and PROFIsafe. The evaluated unit identified itself over the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core interface as product \texttt{ifm-AL400S}, hardware version 1.4.0.2, hardware revision AA, and software revision 1.1.4.888. All reported results refer exclusively to this configuration.

Following the asset classification of Section \ref{subsec:assets_attack_surfaces}, the \gls{acr:SRIO} is decomposed into hardware, software, and data assets. For the practical assessment, the externally accessible attack surface was divided into the \gls{acr:HTTP}-based \gls{acr:IoT}-Core diagnostic interface, the \gls{acr:PROFINET} and PROFIsafe communication path, the \gls{acr:DCP} configuration functions, and the firmware update mechanism. The first two form, respectively, the \gls{acr:IT}-side diagnostic path and the \gls{acr:OT}-side fieldbus path. The \gls{acr:DCP} and firmware functions are configuration-time extensions of the same non-safety path. Only the interfaces accessible from the implemented \gls{acr:NV1} position were exercised. Internal debug and inter-processor interfaces, such as the \gls{acr:SysCom} link between the \gls{acr:COM} and \gls{acr:SCPU} modules and the \gls{acr:JTAG} interface, are included in the asset model as hardware assets but were not accessible within the practical test scope. The \gls{acr:JTAG} pads are sealed by the overmoulded housing of the \gls{acr:SRIO}, and the \gls{acr:SysCom} link is an internal inter-processor bus with no externally exposed connector. Their absence from the practical scope is therefore a physical-access constraint.

The device documentation defines organisational preconditions for secure operation. The intended use assumes the device in a protected zone behind a firewall. A firmware update is expected to be performed by a trusted person behind that firewall. A security risk assessment is required. The debug interface is primarily a physical interface. These preconditions bound the threat model of the practical tests and are relevant to the compensating-countermeasure allocation. Zone protection, trusted update procedures, and the system-level security risk assessment may address obligations that the component does not implement itself. They therefore inform the interpretation of component-capability findings in Section \ref{sec:requirement_verification}.



\subsection{Practical Coverage and Limitations} 
\label{subsec:practical_scope} 

The demonstration applied the methodology to the \gls{acr:SRIO} under the \gls{acr:NV1} network position defined in Section \ref{subsec:nv1}. The test system acted as a direct \gls{acr:IP} participant on the shared switch. The passive-observation variant \gls{acr:NV2} and the in-path variant \gls{acr:NV3} were not realised. No mirror or \gls{acr:SPAN} port was configured because the available laboratory switch does not support port mirroring, and the test system was never placed inline, because rebuilding the setup for an inline bridge exceeded the available time. The cyclic \gls{acr:PROFINET} and PROFIsafe communication between the controller and the \gls{acr:SUT} was therefore not observed, and no claim is made about that traffic.

The empirical coverage was bounded by the single test object, the implemented topology, the available interfaces, and the \gls{acr:NV1}-compatible activities. Within \gls{acr:NV1}, the evaluation focused on the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} interface and on tests in which the test system acted as an unauthorised communication partner. Where a company tool assumed an interface that the device does not expose, the test was recorded as an applicability finding rather than as a failure, following Section \ref{subsec:company_framework}. The scope of this practical validation must be distinguished from the achievement of the thesis objective, which is to develop and validate a systematic methodology for verifying security functions rather than to execute every technically possible test.

\subsection{Requirement, Asset and Interface Mapping}
\label{subsec:req_asset_interface}

This section instantiates, for the \gls{acr:SRIO}, the abstract asset-and-threat mapping step defined in Section \ref{subsec:asset_threat_mapping}. Each of the fourteen derived requirements (Table \ref{tab:mvo-requirements}) is bound to the concrete asset it protects, the \gls{acr:STRIDE} threat it defends against, and the security function that provides the defence. These three columns follow the order of the traceability chain defined in Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model}. Table \ref{tab:req_asset_interface} records the resulting mapping. It summarises, at requirement level, the working test-case catalogue introduced in Section \ref{subsec:test_specification}. The individual abstract scenarios that this summary aggregates, including the concrete interface, the network access variant, and the evidence for each, are reported by security category in Section \ref{sec:executed_tests}, and the executed subset is assessed in Section \ref{sec:requirement_verification}.

The columns are read as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Asset:} the entries of Table \ref{tab:assets} affected by the requirement. A requirement typically protects more than one asset.
	\item \textbf{Threat:} the \gls{acr:STRIDE} categories exercised across the requirement's test scenarios. A requirement is often examined from more than one threat angle. For example, RQ-001 is tested against an impersonated device (Spoofing), a manipulated frame (Tampering), and a flooding condition (Denial of Service). Following Section \ref{subsec:test_specification}, a scenario with no adversarial goal, such as a pure functional or identification baseline, does not contribute a threat entry.
	\item \textbf{Security function:} for each threat listed, the function of Section \ref{subsec:security_functions} that addresses it, following the \gls{acr:STRIDE}-to-function mapping of Table \ref{tab:threat_function} (for example, Spoofing pairs with Authentication, Information Disclosure with Confidentiality and Encryption). Where a requirement concerns discovery rather than a protective mechanism, this is instead the identification-and-inventory function that Table \ref{tab:category_function_fr} also grounds in Section \ref{subsec:security_functions}.
\end{itemize}

The required network access variant (Section \ref{sec:test_environment}) is not a column of Table \ref{tab:req_asset_interface}. Access is not a property of the requirement, the asset, or the threat, but rather of the individual test technique chosen to verify a security function. It is the last element of the traceability chain to be fixed, following Test Objective and Test Technique in Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model}. Different scenarios for the same requirement can therefore require different access variants, so the position is decided case by case for each concrete test, and is reported alongside that test in Section \ref{sec:executed_tests}.

The underlying derivation chain from the Regulation through prEN 50742 and \gls{acr:IEC} 62443-4-2 is documented in Appendix \ref{app:requirement_derivation}.

\section{Test Execution and Results}
\label{sec:executed_tests} 
The two-level test catalogue derived in Chapter \ref{chap:methodology} defines abstract test scenarios for all fourteen requirements, including the tests that depend on the passive-observation variant \gls{acr:NV2} and the in-path variant \gls{acr:NV3}. Only a subset of these was executed in the practical phase, the applicable company tests and the requirement-oriented tests that the \gls{acr:NV1} position supports. This section reports the seven security categories of Table \ref{tab:security_categories} in the same order as Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}, so that the abstract mapping, the selected method, and the executed result can be read together. The tests that require \gls{acr:NV2} or \gls{acr:NV3} are described as designed but not executed. Every reported result is limited to what the stored evidence shows. A tool execution is not treated as requirement verification, and an expected outcome from a test specification is not presented as an observed result.

Each category is introduced by the same reasoning: the machine-level obligation of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 from which the addressed component requirements are derived, what must therefore be checked on the \gls{acr:SRIO}, how that property can be tested and which company countermeasure applies, and the general finding on the device, grounded in the stored evidence. Every performed test is stored in some form as evidence, depending on the test as a text file, an \gls{acr:HTML} report, a log file, or a screenshot. The exact tool commands for the executed activities are listed in Appendix \ref{app:tool_commands}.


\subsection{Identification and Asset Discovery}
\label{subsec:cat_identification}

Annex III, Section 1.1.9 requires that safety-critical software and data be identified (RQ-004, RQ-006), that this identification be available at all times in an easily accessible form (RQ-007), and that the connection of another device does not lead to a hazardous situation (RQ-001). This category therefore examines whether the \gls{acr:SRIO} exposes a discoverable, enumerable non-safety attack surface, and whether it can produce accurate hardware, software, and firmware identification data on request, at product granularity. Nmap (CM-001) and Nikto (CM-007) enumerate the reachable non-safety services, and a scripted query of the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \texttt{deviceinfo} and \texttt{firmware} endpoints reads the identification data directly. Both run under \gls{acr:NV1} (Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}). The \gls{acr:SBOM} completeness analysis and the data-signature uniqueness check belong to the abstract catalogue but were not executed. A complete \gls{acr:SBOM} is the practical expression of the component-inventory capability (\gls{acr:CR} 7.8 \cite[pp. 68]{IEC62443-4-2}) to which RQ-004, RQ-006, and RQ-007 are mapped (Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_iec62443}). It would list every installed software and firmware component together with its version, rather than only the top-level identifiers that a product-granularity read exposes. The activities reported below show which services are reachable and confirm that safety-relevant software is identifiable at product granularity, but they do not establish a complete \gls{acr:SBOM}, and they do not support a hazardous-situation judgement.

\paragraph{Attack-Surface Enumeration}
\label{subsec:res_attack_surface}

This activity examined the externally reachable services on the non-safety path. It relates to CM-001 and CM-007, to requirement RQ-001, and to asset F, the separation of the safety and the non-safety domain. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} path and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_attacksurface}.

A full \gls{acr:TCP} port scan and a top-ports \gls{acr:UDP} scan were run against the \gls{acr:SUT} with Nmap, each repeated on two separate dates to confirm reproducibility \cite{nmap_usage}. Both \gls{acr:TCP} runs (\texttt{-sS -sV -T3}) covered all 65535 ports and consistently reported one open port, port 80, which returned \gls{acr:HTTP} responses without a server banner. All other \gls{acr:TCP} ports were reported as closed. The open \gls{acr:HTTP} port is consistent with the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core diagnostic interface. Both \gls{acr:UDP} runs of the top 200 ports reported port 161 as open, identified as \gls{acr:SNMP}, and port 49152 as open or filtered. An open \gls{acr:SNMP} port is consistent with a PROFINET device because the PROFINET standard uses \gls{acr:SNMP} for network diagnostics \cite[p. 11]{pi_pn_in_pa_2024}. 

The externally reachable services on the non-safety path are therefore the \gls{acr:HTTP} service on port 80 and the \gls{acr:SNMP} service on port 161. On this device the physical debug interface is encapsulated and not reachable, so the scan characterises only the reachable \gls{acr:HTTP} service surface. The Nikto report lists missing \gls{acr:HTTP} security headers, including content-security-policy, x-content-type-options, strict-transport-security, and permissions-policy. The report also flags several \texttt{/actuator/*} paths as Spring Boot Actuator endpoints that return \gls{acr:JSON}.

Nikto is a generic web-server vulnerability and misconfiguration scanner. It enumerates common web paths and matches responses against known vulnerability signatures \cite{nikto_overview}. Its findings here therefore characterise the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core diagnostic \gls{acr:HTTP} service, which is the device's non-safety web interface by design, and not the physical debug interface discussed in Section \ref{subsec:test_object}, which remains encapsulated and outside the practical scope.

The device is an ifm \gls{acr:IoT}-Core and not a Spring Boot application, and its \gls{acr:HTTP} service returns a generic \gls{acr:JSON} structure for many paths, which a generic scanner signature can misclassify as an exposed Actuator endpoint. The finding therefore does not, on its own, indicate a risk. Directly requesting one such path and inspecting the raw response remains an open verification item; no result is claimed here. %TODO Quelle hierfür

The enumeration supports RQ-001 at the level of the non-safety path. It shows which services a connecting device can reach. It does not, on its own, demonstrate a hazardous situation. The \gls{acr:SNMP} service on port 161 was deliberately excluded from the practical scope because it was not examined beyond enumeration. Its authentication, information-disclosure and logging implications remain open items for a future test campaign.

\paragraph{Identification-Surface Inventory}
\label{subsec:res_identification}
This activity enumerated the identification data that the device exposes. It relates to requirements RQ-004, RQ-006, and RQ-007, and to assets B and D. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \texttt{deviceinfo} and \texttt{firmware} paths and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_inventory}.

For each queried path the device returned a \gls{acr:JSON} response with status code 200 that contained the requested identifier. The inventory supports RQ-004, RQ-006, and RQ-007 at product granularity. It confirms that safety-relevant software is identifiable and available on request. \subsection{Communication Security}
\label{subsec:cat_communication}
Annex III, Section 1.1.9 requires that the connection of another device does not lead to a hazardous situation (RQ-001) and that hardware components transmitting signal or data relevant to connection or access to safety-critical software be protected against corruption (RQ-002). Section 1.2.1 requires that access to tracing-log data be restricted (RQ-014). This category therefore examines whether the safety-relevant communication path is protected from tampering, spoofing, or injection by an unexpected participant, and whether data carried on the reachable non-safety diagnostic path, including the tracing log, is exposed in a way that an unauthorised observer could exploit. RQ-014 is examined here only from this confidentiality-in-transit angle. Whether the interface itself enforces the access restriction to a reasoned conformity request is a separate, access-control angle examined under authentication and access control (Section \ref{subsec:cat_access}). The safety-telegram integrity-rejection, in-path manipulation, and watchdog-passivation scenarios require the \gls{acr:NV2} or \gls{acr:NV3} position or the engineering tool and were designed but not executed. Under \gls{acr:NV1}, an SSLscan probe checks whether the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service uses transport encryption. The original CM-005 procedure is not applicable to this device (Table \ref{tab:cm_applicability}), so the probe is run directly against the tracing-log path and reused from the identification-surface inventory read (Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}). The transport observation reported below is therefore the only activity executed under \gls{acr:NV1}. It also supports RQ-004 as a secondary contribution, alongside its primary categorisation under identification and asset discovery (Section \ref{subsec:cat_identification}). Had a \gls{acr:TLS} endpoint been present and correctly configured, it would have provided confidentiality and integrity for the transported identification and tracing-log data against a passive or active on-path observer, closing the defence-in-depth gap that the absence of \gls{acr:TLS} otherwise leaves open \cite{ietf-tls-rfc8446bis-10}.

\paragraph{Cleartext Transmission of the Identification and Tracing-Log Data}
\label{subsec:res_cleartext}
This activity examined the transport protection of the identification data and of the tracing-log data. It relates to CM-005, to requirements RQ-004 and RQ-014, and to assets D and I. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service, including the \texttt{errorlog/loglist} path that carries the tracing-log content, and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_cleartext}.

SSLscan verifies the \gls{acr:TLS} configuration of a \gls{acr:TLS} endpoint. It probes for an SSL or \gls{acr:TLS} handshake on a given host and port and reports the enabled protocol versions and the certificate \Cite{rbsec-sslscan} \cite{kali_sslscan}. The tool was directed at the \gls{acr:HTTP} service on port 80. The saved output shows that no SSL or \gls{acr:TLS} protocol version, from SSLv2 through TLSv1.3, is enabled, and that no certificate can be parsed. The output therefore confirms that port 80 does not provide a \gls{acr:TLS} endpoint, so the service is plain \gls{acr:HTTP} without transport encryption for every path served on it. The identification data of Section \ref{subsec:res_identification} observed in the test system's own requests and responses was therefore transmitted in plaintext.

The same absence of transport encryption applies to the tracing-log path. A dedicated SSLscan run against the identical \gls{acr:HTTP} service, saved separately as evidence for RQ-014, confirms that the tracing log read at \texttt{errorlog/loglist} is served without a \gls{acr:TLS} option. A packet capture intended to show the tracing-log content itself in cleartext was planned but was not saved as a stored artefact, so the log-content-specific claim is reported as an unscored observation; the scored finding for RQ-014 is limited to the shared absence of a \gls{acr:TLS} endpoint.
The absence of \gls{acr:TLS} is a device characteristic, not a failed \gls{acr:TLS} test. It is relevant to RQ-004 and to RQ-014's confidentiality-in-transit angle as a defence-in-depth observation.
A confidentiality impact requires two further conditions. The transmitted data must be sensitive, and an unauthorised party must be able to observe it. The second condition depends on the \gls{acr:NV2} position, which was not realised. The confidentiality claim is therefore limited to the cleartext transmission that the test system observed in its own requests and responses.








\subsection{Logging and Traceability}
\label{subsec:cat_logging}
Annex III, Section 1.1.9 requires evidence of any intervention in hardware components relevant for connection or access to safety-critical software (RQ-003), evidence of any intervention in the installed software (RQ-008), and evidence of any modification of the installed software or its configuration (RQ-009). Section 1.2.1 requires a tracing log of intervention data retained for five years (RQ-012). This category therefore examines whether the device generates, retains, and correctly time-references evidence of interventions, and whether that evidence survives a power cycle and remains attributable over the mandated retention period. This time-reference expectation derives from the auditable-events capability (\gls{acr:CR} 2.8/2.11 \cite[p. 47, p. 49]{IEC62443-4-2}) and its associated timestamp property, to which RQ-003, RQ-008, RQ-009, and RQ-012 are mapped (Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_iec62443}). No company countermeasure targets logging directly. The selected method is a requirement-oriented scenario that reads the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core error log and systick counter before and after an induced event and a cold restart (Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}). The software-installation and configuration-modification evidence scenarios for RQ-008 and RQ-009 require an induced software or configuration change. Only the baseline was read, so they were not fully executed. The intervention-evidence and time-attribution activity reported below is the only fully executed scenario in this category and yields the two Fail verdicts of Table \ref{tab:req_verification}.

\paragraph{Intervention Evidence and Time Attribution}
\label{subsec:res_evidence}
This activity examined what evidence the device records for an intervention and how it is time-referenced. It relates to requirements RQ-003, RQ-008, and RQ-012, and to assets I and G. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \texttt{errorlog} and \texttt{systick} paths and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_evidence}.

The machine-level requirement requires a tracing-log capability, and usable intervention evidence requires a time reference that remains unambiguous across the relevant operating states. No dedicated tracing log was exposed through the examined interface. The available substitute artefact is an error log with a relative system tick, and it is cleared at a cold restart. The RQ-003 verdict therefore rests on a capability finding rather than on the specific event induced. The deficiency is the structural absence of a dedicated tracing-log function and the demonstrated inadequacy of the only available substitute artefact, and both properties hold independently of which intervention is triggered. This is a different situation from RQ-008 and RQ-009, whose acceptance conditions turn on whether a particular software-installation or configuration event is recorded, and which therefore remain Not Assessed until such an event is induced. The induced input fault described below is used only to elicit and characterise the behaviour of the substitute artefact; it is not treated as a stand-in for an intervention in a connection-relevant hardware component. The activity therefore records both the apparent absence of the required tracing-log function and the properties of the substitute artefact.

The error log and the system tick counter were read over \gls{acr:HTTP} in a controlled sequence. The sequence recorded a baseline, an induced input fault by short-circuiting pins 1 and 3 of a digital input of the \gls{acr:SRIO}, a cold restart of the device by removing power, and a second induced input fault. At the baseline the error log was empty. The short-circuit at the digital input triggered a fault, which produced two error entries on the safety \gls{acr:CPU}. \subsection{Availability and Resilience}
\label{subsec:cat_availability} 
Annex III, Section 1.1.9 requires that identification information of safety-relevant installed software be available at all times (RQ-007). Section 1.2.1 requires that the control system withstand reasonably foreseeable malicious attempts that could lead to a hazardous situation (RQ-010). Both requirements concern the availability of the \gls{acr:SRIO} in general, including its safety function under a \gls{acr:DoS} condition. The practical scope here is bounded to the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} interface under \gls{acr:NV1}, so the activities reported below characterise only the non-safety diagnostic path and do not, by themselves, establish availability of the safety function itself.
This category therefore examines whether the diagnostic \gls{acr:HTTP} service remains available under connection load and within its specified operating envelope (RQ-007), and whether the device resists a network or link-layer flood without the disturbance propagating to the safety function (RQ-010, domain-separation aspect). Siege (CM-009) load-tests the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service at the framework default of 50 connections and near the device's specified two-connection envelope at 3 connections and a manual connection-hold procedure occupies both permitted slots and probes a third request. A network and link-layer flood (hping3, macof) was attempted but not captured as evidence. The domain-separation confirmation itself requires the \gls{acr:NV2} position and was designed but not executed \cite{macof_man} \cite{kali_hping3}. The load and connection-hold activity reported below produces outside-envelope or Not-Assessed observations. The layer-two flood produced no usable evidence.

\paragraph{Availability under Connection Load}
\label{subsec:res_availability}
This activity examined the availability of the identification interface under connection load. It relates to CM-009, to requirements RQ-007 and RQ-010, and to assets H and F. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_availability}.

A load test with Siege was run against the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service at 50 and at 3 concurrent connections. The two load levels reflect the framework default and a near-envelope stress case for the \gls{acr:SRIO}, whose requirement documentation permits at most two concurrent connections. The console output and the Siege summary logs are stored as evidence \cite{joedog-siege}.

The acceptance criterion is bounded by the specified operating envelope. Availability is assessed only for the number of concurrent connections that the product specification permits. Both the 3-connection and 50-connection runs are therefore recorded as observations outside the device range. Neither can, by itself, support a Pass or Fail verdict for the specified operating condition.

The stored summaries show that the service responded to a large number of requests with \gls{acr:HTTP} status 200 and that a substantial number of connections failed in the same runs. The 50-connection runs recorded 1,273 successful transactions and 407 failed connections in total. One run completed no transaction and failed all 50 connection attempts. The 3-connection run recorded 332 successful transactions and no failed connections. These results document behaviour under the tested loads, but the tested loads exceeded the specified maximum and therefore do not establish availability within the product's declared operating envelope. Whether operating beyond the declared envelope nonetheless constitutes a security-relevant weakness is a design question for the manufacturer rather than a component-level verdict within this methodology. The two-connection limit is a specified operating constraint, and demonstrating resilience beyond a specified limit was not an objective of this test.


The connection-hold procedure was executed against the identification interface. The job listing shows two connection-holding processes running as intended. While both connections were held, a third request to the identification endpoint did not receive a response within the five-second timeout. After one held connection was released, the same request again returned \gls{acr:HTTP} status 200. The evidence therefore demonstrates the intended connection-hold condition and recovery of the endpoint after a slot became available. The third request was made while the two permitted concurrent connections were already occupied and is consequently an outside-envelope overload observation, not a direct test of the specified operating condition. The activity remains Not Assessed for the requirement because it exercised only one endpoint and does not establish availability of all required identification information under every permitted operating condition.


The load results and the connection-hold attempt do not, by themselves, support a Pass or Fail verdict. A statement about domain separation or about the safety function is not possible under \gls{acr:NV1} because the safety channel was not observed.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{subsec:cat_robustness}
Section 1.2.1 requires that the control system withstand reasonably foreseeable malicious attempts that could lead to a hazardous situation (RQ-010), examined here specifically under malformed input rather than under volume load. This category therefore examines whether the \gls{acr:HTTP} service maintains its specified behaviour when it receives malformed requests, and whether any resulting disruption remains confined to the non-safety interface. boofuzz (CM-010) fuzzes the request line of an \gls{acr:HTTP} GET request against the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core diagnostic service under \gls{acr:NV1} (Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}) \cite{boofuzz_quickstart}. The fieldbus-discovery and safety-frame fuzzing scenarios require the \gls{acr:NV2} or \gls{acr:NV3} position and were designed but not executed. The activity reported below shows a temporary service disruption and an unstored fault-indication observation; neither, by itself, supports a Pass or Fail verdict for RQ-010.

\paragraph{Robustness under Malformed Input}
\label{subsec:res_robustness}
This activity examined the robustness of the \gls{acr:HTTP} service against malformed input. It relates to CM-010, to requirement RQ-010, and to asset H. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core \gls{acr:HTTP} service and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_robustness}.

A fuzzing test was run with boofuzz against the \gls{acr:HTTP} service on port 80. The saved script fuzzes the request line of an \gls{acr:HTTP} GET request. The boofuzz session databases are stored as evidence.
The session record shows successful processing of the initial test cases, followed by a failed reconnection after a subsequent malformed request. The \gls{acr:HTTP} service became reachable again approximately 41 seconds later. The triggering input and the internal cause of the interruption were not established.

During the run, the device visibly indicated a fault condition on its status indicators. The indication changed as the run continued and had not fully cleared after the test was aborted. A manual read of the error log taken after the run showed one additional \texttt{FATAL}-level entry and three additional \texttt{ERROR}-level entries that were not present in the baseline of Section \ref{subsec:res_evidence}. This reading was not captured to a stored file and is therefore reported as an observation rather than as evidence.
Independently of the specific error codes involved, which are not interpreted here, the indicator and error-log behaviour together show that the device entered a fault state as a consequence of the \gls{acr:HTTP}-level fuzzing. This is not expected behaviour and warrants closer review by the development department. Because the observation concerns safety monitoring (asset E), it is also relevant to the domain-separation obligation of RQ-001 (Section \ref{subsec:res_attack_surface}, asset F). It suggests, without yet proving, that the disruption is not confined to the non-safety interface.

The stored evidence therefore shows a temporary disruption of the \gls{acr:HTTP} service on the non-safety path under malformed input, followed by recovery, together with an unstored observation of a device fault indication. The disruption requires follow-up by development. Its causation, reproducibility, and effect on the safety function are not established because the safety channel was not monitored and the fault indication was not captured as a stored artefact. The activity therefore supports an observation but not a complete requirement verdict for RQ-010. The requirement remains Not Assessed under the conservative aggregation rule. An unresolved device fault state of this kind falls within the scope of \gls{acr:IEC} 62443-4-1's Defect Management practice. Under the governance model of Appendix \ref{app:SecureDevelopmentLifecycle}, it would be escalated to Software/Firmware Development in a consulted role and tracked by Security Engineering. Whether this specific finding has been formally raised through that process is outside the scope of this thesis and is noted here as an open item for the responsible team.



\subsection{Authentication and Access Control}
\label{subsec:cat_access}
Section 1.2.1 requires that modifications to safety-relevant settings or rules that could lead to a hazardous situation be prevented (RQ-011) and that access to tracing-log data be restricted to a reasoned conformity request (RQ-014). Section 1.1.9's requirement to collect evidence of modification of the installed software or configuration (RQ-009) is also examined here because the same write attempts that probe access control also serve as its modification-evidence baseline. This category therefore examines whether the device authenticates or authorises a connecting entity before permitting a modification, whether it restricts access to the tracing-log data, and whether an unauthorised write attempt is visibly rejected. \gls{acr:OWASP} ZAP is an active web-application scanner that probes an \gls{acr:HTTP} interface for common vulnerabilities and misconfigurations, including access-control and privilege-related issues; Hydra is a network-authentication brute-force tool that attempts credential-based logon against an exposed authentication endpoint \cite{zaproxy_getting_started}. \gls{acr:OWASP} ZAP (CM-004) actively scans the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core web interface. Hydra (CM-003) is not applicable because the device exposes no authentication endpoint (Table \ref{tab:cm_applicability}) \cite{kali_hydra}.
Under \gls{acr:NV1}, a write probe posts to three \gls{acr:IoT}-Core nodes. The unauthenticated configuration-protocol reset and the physical-barrier scenarios were designed but not executed. The activity reported below confirms a single anonymous read-only role and shows that three specific write requests were rejected, but the rejection does not distinguish input validation from an authorisation decision.

\paragraph{Access Control and Configuration Interfaces} 
\label{subsec:res_access_control}
This activity examined the access-control model of the non-safety interface. It relates to CM-003 and CM-004, to requirements RQ-011, RQ-014, and RQ-009, and to assets B and G. It targets the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core interface and the configuration protocol and was run under \gls{acr:NV1}. The commands are listed in Appendix \ref{app:cmd_access}.

The \gls{acr:IoT}-Core interface provided anonymous read access without a login. CM-003 was not applicable because no authentication endpoint was exposed. No Hydra execution is claimed. An active web scan was run with \gls{acr:OWASP} ZAP through a local proxy, and the report was saved. A comparison of multiple user roles was not applicable because the interface exposes a single anonymous read-only role.

A write probe was run against three \gls{acr:IoT}-Core nodes. A POST request to the safety-address node, to a digital-output node, and to the application-tag node was rejected in each case, and the responses are stored as evidence. The device answered with a bad-request response rather than an explicit authorisation denial. A bad-request response shows that the write was not accepted, but it does not by itself prove an access-control decision because it can also result from the request format. For the application-tag node, which the device documentation lists as writable, an explicit authorisation denial would have been the expected response.

The anonymous read-only access is an observation about the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core interface. It does not, on its own, decide RQ-011 or RQ-014. The three submitted write requests did not result in an accepted modification, but the returned bad-request responses do not distinguish input validation from an authorisation decision. The activity therefore does not demonstrate an access-control mechanism. It does not cover the full write surface, the configuration-protocol behaviour, or the factory-reset path, which were not exercised. The tracing-log access control of RQ-014 is not evidenced beyond the single anonymous read-only role. The requirements therefore remain \textbf{Not Assessed} under the conservative aggregation rule.

\label{subsec:cat_config_firmware}
Section 1.1.9 requires that hardware components transmitting signal or data relevant to connection or access to safety-critical software be protected against corruption (the parameter-integrity aspect of RQ-002) and that identified safety-critical software and data be protected against corruption (RQ-005). This category therefore examines whether firmware and configuration data are protected against corruption or forgery, whether an unsigned or modified firmware package can be installed, and whether the physical address and mode interface resists tampering. None of the ten company tests targets this category directly (Table \ref{tab:cm_tc_map}). \section{Requirement Assessment Results}
\label{sec:requirement_verification}

The purpose of this section is to show that the methodology can assign a traceable verdict to each derived component-level requirement from the collected evidence. The per-requirement results are the output of applying the method to one device under the \gls{acr:NV1} position. They are not a conformance audit of the product, and no legal conformity is claimed. A Fail verdict denotes an evidenced absence of component capability against a derived requirement, not a declaration of legal non-conformity of the product. Under the allocation defined in Section \ref{subsec:regulatory_applicability}, the corresponding machine-level obligation may instead be satisfied by a compensating countermeasure at system or zone level, which the machine manufacturer or integrator must document. Section \ref{subsec:req_verification_table} relates the executed activities of Section \ref{sec:executed_tests} to the fourteen derived requirements and assigns a verdict according to the scale of Section \ref{subsec:verdicts}. Section \ref{subsec:metrics_application} summarises the resulting coverage of the practical phase as direct counts.

\subsection{Coverage of the Practical Phase}
\label{subsec:metrics_application} 
The assessment model of Section \ref{sec:evaluation_metrics} is qualitative, so the results are summarised as direct counts rather than as ratios. Fourteen requirements were derived from the Regulation. Of the ten company tests, five were applicable and executed, three were not applicable to the device or topology, and one was blocked in the laboratory. One further procedure was considered applicable but the original test case was not usable as a direct security verdict because it primarily produced a transport-level observation. An activity is counted once only when a stored artefact supports its interpretation. Unsupported observations and planned procedures are excluded. Two requirements, RQ-003 and RQ-012, are assessed as Fail on the basis of evidenced component-capability deviations. The remaining requirements are Not Assessed because their complete acceptance conditions were not covered.
The evidence base includes raw Nmap and Nikto output, SSLscan output, inventory reads, error-log and system-tick reads, Siege summaries, connection-hold output, write-probe responses, boofuzz session data, and the \gls{acr:OWASP} ZAP report. These artefacts support reproducible observations, but not necessarily complete requirement verification. The limited number of definitive verdicts reflects the bounded scope of the practical phase. The assessment can be extended when the outstanding \gls{acr:NV2}, \gls{acr:NV3}, firmware, version-history, and engineering-tool activities are executed and recorded.



\section{Discussion of Results}
\label{sec:discussion} 
Section \ref{sec:requirement_verification} assigned a verdict to each of the fourteen derived requirements. This section steps back from the individual verdicts and interprets the resulting pattern as a whole.

\subsection{Synthesis of Findings Across Test Categories}
\label{subsec:discussion_synthesis} 
Table \ref{tab:req_verification} shows a pattern rather than an isolated result. Two requirements are assessed as Fail, and the remaining twelve are Not Assessed. Under the conservative aggregation rule of Section \ref{subsec:requirement_assessment}, a Not Assessed verdict does not indicate that a security function is absent. It indicates that the executed evidence does not cover the complete acceptance condition of the requirement. The predominance of this verdict is therefore not primarily a finding about the device, but a direct consequence of the scope decisions documented in Section \ref{subsec:practical_scope}. The practical phase realised only the direct-access variant \gls{acr:NV1}, while a majority of the abstract test scenarios in Section \ref{sec:executed_tests} that could have produced a Pass or a further Fail depend on \gls{acr:NV2}, \gls{acr:NV3}, an induced software or configuration change, or the engineering tool. This is visible category by category. In identification and asset discovery (Section \ref{subsec:cat_identification}), top-level firmware was identified, but third-party components necessary for safe operation, such as the communication stack and the real-time operating system, remain unidentified, so complete software coverage was not established for RQ-004 and RQ-006. RQ-007's related question is distinct and availability-only. Whether the identification information can be retrieved at all times was tested through the load and connection-hold activity of Section \ref{subsec:cat_availability}, but only at one endpoint outside the specified operating envelope. In availability and resilience and in robustness (Sections \ref{subsec:cat_availability}, \ref{subsec:cat_robustness}), the load test and the fuzzing activity produced observations, but neither could be related to the cyclic safety channel, which only \gls{acr:NV2} exposes. In authentication and access control (Section \ref{subsec:cat_access}), the write probe demonstrated that three specific requests were rejected, but a bad-request response does not distinguish input validation from an authorisation decision, so the requirement remains open rather than confirmed.

The two Fail verdicts, RQ-003 and RQ-012, stand apart from this pattern because they were fully assessable within \gls{acr:NV1} alone. Both concern the same substitute artefact, the \gls{acr:IoT}-Core error log, and both are demonstrated by the same evidence sequence in Table \ref{tab:rq003_evidence}. The log carries only a relative system tick and no actor identity, and its content is deleted by a cold restart. This is the strongest finding of the practical phase, precisely because it did not require passive observation of the safety channel or an in-path position to demonstrate. It illustrates a general property of the methodology. A requirement can be conclusively assessed under a restricted network position when the demonstrating property lies entirely on the non-safety, \gls{acr:IP}-reachable path. Conversely, requirements whose acceptance condition touches the cyclic \gls{acr:PROFINET}/PROFIsafe communication, such as the domain-separation aspect of RQ-001 or the resilience aspect of RQ-010, cannot be conclusively assessed under \gls{acr:NV1} regardless of how many \gls{acr:NV1}-compatible tests are executed against them because the observation point itself is insufficient for the claim.

The unrealised \gls{acr:NV2} and \gls{acr:NV3} variants therefore do not merely reduce the number of executed test cases. They remove the observation points required for entire classes of claims, specifically those concerning the safety-relevant process data and in-path manipulation. This distinguishes two different reasons for a Not Assessed verdict that the aggregation rule of Section \ref{subsec:requirement_assessment} treats alike but that carry different implications for future work. A requirement can be open because the induced event was never triggered under an otherwise sufficient access position (for example RQ-008 and RQ-009, where only baseline evidence was collected), or it can be open because the required access position itself was never established (for example the domain-separation aspect of RQ-001 and RQ-010). The first class can be closed by extending the test execution within the existing \gls{acr:NV1} topology. The second class requires the topology itself to be extended, specifically a mirror or \gls{acr:SPAN} port for \gls{acr:NV2} or an inline bridge for \gls{acr:NV3}, as noted in Section \ref{subsec:practical_scope}.


\subsection{Answering the Research Questions}
\label{subsec:discussion_rq} 
\textbf{RQ1: How can the machine-level cybersecurity obligations of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 be translated into verifiable, derived component-level security tests for a safety-related industrial gateway?} 

The instantiation in Section \ref{subsec:req_asset_interface} demonstrates that the derivation chain defined in Section \ref{subsec:derivation_mapping}, from the Regulation through prEN 50742 to \gls{acr:IEC} 62443-4-2, and further through the asset and threat mapping of Section \ref{subsec:asset_threat_mapping}, produces requirements that are concrete enough to bind to a specific interface, asset, and network access variant on a real device (Table \ref{tab:req_asset_interface}). The subsequent execution in Section \ref{sec:executed_tests} confirms that each requirement can be operationalised into at least one test activity, even where the activity ultimately yields a Not Assessed verdict rather than a Pass. The two Fail verdicts, RQ-003 and RQ-012, show that the chain is capable of producing a conclusive negative verdict when the acceptance condition lies within the realised access position. RQ1 is therefore answered affirmatively within the scope of \gls{acr:NV1}. The translation from a machine-level obligation to a verifiable, evidence-based component test is demonstrated end to end, though the demonstration is fully conclusive only for the subset of requirements whose acceptance condition does not depend on \gls{acr:NV2} or \gls{acr:NV3}. 

\textbf{RQ2: Which tests of the existing company framework are applicable to an industrial gateway, and how can non-applicable tests be identified before execution?} 

Table \ref{tab:cm_applicability} answers this question directly. Of the ten company tests, five were applicable and executed (CM-001, CM-004, CM-007, CM-009, CM-010), three were not applicable to the device or the topology (CM-002, CM-003, CM-006), one original procedure was not applicable while the tool itself produced a usable interface observation (CM-005), and one was blocked by the laboratory environment (CM-008). The applicability assessment of Section \ref{subsec:cm_applicability} identified all three not-applicable cases before execution, on the basis of an explicit tool-to-target check. CM-002 assumes a second network zone that the flat topology does not provide, CM-003 assumes an authentication endpoint that the \gls{acr:SUT} does not expose, and CM-006 assumes an operating-system shell that the embedded device does not expose. RQ2 is therefore answered by the methodology itself. A systematic applicability assessment, performed prior to execution and grounded in the assumed interface of each tool rather than in the tool's general purpose, correctly separates applicable tests from tests whose assumed target interface is known in advance to be absent. This distinction is what prevents a not-applicable outcome from being misread as a passed or failed security test. 

\textbf{RQ3: What can direct-access testing at the physical \gls{acr:IT}/\gls{acr:OT} interface verify about the effectiveness of the implemented security functions, and where are its boundaries?} 

The synthesis in Section \ref{subsec:discussion_synthesis} bounds this answer. Direct-access testing under \gls{acr:NV1} can conclusively verify requirements whose acceptance condition is exercised entirely through the \gls{acr:IP}-reachable non-safety path, as demonstrated by the Fail verdicts for RQ-003 and RQ-012. It can also produce reproducible interface-level observations, such as the absence of a \gls{acr:TLS} endpoint (Section \ref{subsec:cat_communication}) or the absence of an authentication endpoint (Section \ref{subsec:cat_access}), that constrain but do not by themselves decide a requirement. Its boundary is equally precise: \gls{acr:NV1} cannot observe the cyclic \gls{acr:PROFINET}/PROFIsafe communication between controller and \gls{acr:SUT}, so it cannot support any claim about the integrity of safety-relevant process data in transit, about in-path manipulation, or about the effect of a network disturbance on the safety function itself. Every requirement in Table \ref{tab:req_verification} whose limitation column names the safety channel, the \gls{acr:NV2} position, or the \gls{acr:NV3} position marks exactly this boundary. RQ3 is therefore answered as follows: direct-access testing is sufficient to verify security functions that are fully exposed on the \gls{acr:IT}-side interface, but it structurally cannot verify or refute claims about the \gls{acr:OT}-side safety communication, regardless of the number or the depth of the tests executed at the \gls{acr:IT}/\gls{acr:OT} interface.
