\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

Building upon the theoretical foundations established in Chapter \ref{chap:fundamentals}, this chapter develops a practical security testing methodology. It translates abstract regulatory requirements into a verifiable engineering framework. 

First, Section \ref{sec:research_design} outlines the overarching research design and defines the methodological scope. Section \ref{sec:traceability} explains the systematic derivation of test cases to ensure full regulatory traceability. Next, Section \ref{sec:test_environment} designs the physical and logical test environment. Section \ref{sec:execution_governance} defines the execution procedure and the required evidence collection. Finally, Section \ref{sec:evaluation_metrics} establishes quantitative metrics to evaluate the framework's effectiveness and regulatory compliance.


\section{Research Design and Methodological Scope}
\label{sec:research_design}

To systematically address the challenge of verifying cybersecurity compliance for industrial gateways, this research follows the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) proposed by \citeauthor{Peffers}. DSRM provides a structured process for solving practical engineering problems through the creation and evaluation of innovative artifacts \cite[p. 46]{Peffers}. In this thesis, the primary artifact is a standards-based functional security testing framework. This framework enables the traceable, repeatable, and systematic verification of security functions at the IT/OT interface.

The six fundamental activities of the DSRM process map directly to the structure of this thesis:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Problem Identification and Motivation:} The lack of structured testing approaches for the new cybersecurity obligations under Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 is established in Chapter \ref{chap:introduction}.
	\item \textbf{Definition of Objectives:} The need for a traceable, standards-based verification framework utilizing prEN 50742 and the IEC 62443 series is defined in Chapter \ref{chap:introduction}.
	\item \textbf{Design and Development:} This core phase is addressed in the current chapter (Chapter \ref{chap:methodology}) by designing the testing framework, the environment, and the execution governance.
	\item \textbf{Demonstration:} The practical application of the developed framework to a physical industrial gateway (System Under Test) is conducted in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}.
	\item \textbf{Evaluation:} The framework's effectiveness and fulfillment of the regulatory documentation requirements are quantitatively assessed in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}.
	\item \textbf{Communication:} The documentation, critical discussion, and dissemination of the research results are achieved through this thesis.
\end{enumerate}

To ensure valid and reproducible results, the scope of this methodology must be strictly delimited. The proposed framework focuses exclusively on \textbf{functional security testing}. It verifies whether explicitly planned security mechanisms (e.g., authentication, access control) operate correctly and fulfill their normative purpose \cite[p. 82]{1341418}.
Consequently, exploratory vulnerability discovery, such as unstructured penetration testing or automated fuzzing, remains explicitly out of scope. While these techniques are valuable for finding unknown software bugs, they cannot systematically prove the existence and correct operation of mandatory security controls required by Regulation (EU) 2023/1230.


\section{Systematic Requirement Engineering and Traceability}
\label{sec:traceability}
The first phase of the methodology translates abstract legal obligations into verifiable engineering test cases. A systematic approach is crucial to ensure no regulatory requirement is overlooked and every executed test serves a distinct compliance purpose.

\subsection{Regulatory Derivation and Asset Mapping}
\label{subsec:derivation_mapping}
To bridge the gap between Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 and practical engineering, the methodology requires operationalizing abstract legal texts into testable criteria. This derivation process follows a strict three-step procedure:

First, precise cybersecurity requirements are systematically extracted from the high-level legal text of the Machinery Regulation. This step is necessary to isolate specific, verifiable obligations from general legal prose (the practical application of this step yields the 14 requirements documented in Appendix \ref{app:sec:mvo_requirements}).

Second, the procedure maps these extracted obligations to the harmonized standard draft prEN 50742. This intermediate step is crucial because the regulation only defines overarching safety goals, whereas prEN 50742 provides the detailed technical guidance required for machinery design (see the resulting mapping in Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_pren50742}).

Finally, the framework translates these guidelines into concrete Component Requirements (CRs) of IEC 62443-4-2. This final translation is mandatory to obtain granular, component-level engineering specifications tailored specifically to industrial network devices (detailed in Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_iec62443}).

However, abstract standard clauses cannot be tested in isolation. To create executable test cases, the methodology dictates a mandatory asset-mapping step. Before any test can be designed, the specific target system is decomposed into its constituent physical and logical assets (e.g., hardware interfaces, software APIs, or data stores). Each derived requirement is then systematically paired with the relevant assets. This procedural step ensures that it is clearly defined not only \textit{what} needs to be tested, but exactly \textit{where} the corresponding security mechanism must be verified during the practical implementation.

\subsection{Traceability Model}
\label{subsec:traceability_model}
To guarantee legal compliance, the framework employs a strict, unbroken traceability model. Drawing upon established cybersecurity standards (cf. \cite[pp. 36]{DIN_EN_ISO_IEC_15408_1_2024}, \cite[p. 82]{1341418} \cite[p. 21]{DIN_EN_IEC_62443_4_1_2018}), the logical flow of this traceability chain has been structured as follows:

\begin{center}
	Requirement $\rightarrow$ Asset $\rightarrow$ Threat $\rightarrow$ Security Function $\rightarrow$ Test Case $\rightarrow$ Evidence
\end{center}

The individual nodes of this chain are defined as:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Requirement:} The legal obligation (e.g., RQ-011) and its mapped IEC 62443-4-2 control (e.g., CR 2.1).
	\item \textbf{Asset:} The concrete gateway component where the requirement applies.
	\item \textbf{Threat:} The specific attacker goal (derived via \hyperref[subsec:stride]{STRIDE}) that targets the asset.
	\item \textbf{Security Function:} The technical countermeasure implemented to mitigate the threat.
	\item \textbf{Test Case:} The practical procedure used to verify the correct operation of the security function.
	\item \textbf{Evidence:} The recorded artifacts (logs, screenshots) proving the test outcome.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Test Specification}
\label{subsec:test_specification}

To ensure methodological rigor, the test cases are not custom-invented. Instead, they follow a documented and repeatable methodology. The standard IEC 62443-4-1 mandates Security Verification and Validation (SVV) to formally document that all cybersecurity requirements are fulfilled and defense-in-depth strategies operate correctly \cite[p. 37]{DIN_EN_IEC_62443_4_1_2018}. Furthermore, executing security tests on industrial components carries inherent risks. A structured assessment methodology is necessary to provide consistency and minimize these operational disruptions \cite[p. 2-1]{nist_sp800_115}.

While IEC 62443 dictates the normative requirement for verification, the concrete execution procedures rely on specialized industry frameworks. For instance, the OWASP Web Security Testing Guide (WSTG) provides the procedural depth for evaluating web-based interfaces and API assets \cite{manana2026pentest}. Conversely, NIST SP 800-115 serves as the methodological baseline for network-level assessments and infrastructural testing \cite[p. 1-1]{nist_sp800_115}.

To comply with strict documentation requirements and ensure reproducibility, every derived test case is formalized in a standardized data sheet. A structured test case guarantees that another engineer can independently reproduce the exact procedure and verify the expected outcome at a later time \cite[p. 37]{Pilorget2012}. Based on established testing principles, the data sheet comprises the following core elements \cite[p. 15]{Pilorget2012}:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Test ID and Objective:} A unique alphanumeric identifier and a concise definition of the verification goal.
	\item \textbf{Normative Reference:} The direct trace to the corresponding MVO requirement and IEC 62443-4-2 CR to maintain unbroken traceability.
	\item \textbf{Preconditions:} The required initial state of the gateway, such as specific hardware configurations or active network connections.
	\item \textbf{Execution Procedure:} Step-by-step instructions detailing the applied tools (e.g., Nmap, OWASP ZAP) and the injected payloads to enable exact replication.
	\item \textbf{Acceptance Criterion (PASS):} The exact, expected system behavior that constitutes a successful defense (e.g., returning an HTTP 403 error).
\end{itemize}

\section{Design of the Security Test Environment}
\label{sec:test_environment}

Security testing poses a severe risk to the stability of industrial networks. Active vulnerability scanning and malicious payload injection can easily disrupt critical operations. In Operational Technology (OT), system availability and safety represent the highest priorities. Consequently, security tests must never occur within a productive production environment. Establishing a dedicated, isolated test architecture is a mandatory methodological requirement. This approach is advocated by international guidelines, including NIST SP 800-82 \cite[p. 2]{NIST.SP.800-82r3}. 

The primary objective of such a testbed is to demonstrate the application of industrial cybersecurity standards. It provides a controlled platform to measure the performance and resilience of networked control systems under attack \cite[p. 2]{8f3698f787af48ccb0aa063527bbb4de}. To achieve valid results, the test environment must balance strict security isolation with practical operational efficiency.


\subsection{Design of the Testbed}
\label{subsec:architectural_blueprint}

Before determining the specific network isolation strategy, the internal architecture of the test environment must be defined. The credibility of any industrial security testbed heavily depends on its architectural components \cite[p. 4]{DesignAni}. The testbed must incorporate realistic functionality coverage. This ensures that the simulated environment accurately reflects a true industrial setup. 

To achieve a holistic expression of security tests, a valid functional security testbed requires four fundamental architectural zones:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Physical Assets and Field Devices Zone:} This zone represents the actual industrial process. It contains Programmable Logic Controllers (PLCs), sensors, and actuators. PLCs act as the primary control components to locally manage processes through closed-loop feedback \cite[p. 21]{NIST.SP.800-82r3}. Devices deployed in this zone must behave identically to the real system. They must exhibit deterministic scan cycles, accurate response times, and standard industrial protocols such as Modbus/TCP or PROFINET \cite[p. 3]{DevelopMuna}.
	
	\item \textbf{Gateway / System Under Test (SUT) Zone:} The industrial gateway operates at the boundary between the physical process and higher-level IT networks. It collects data from physical devices, supports Machine-to-Machine (M2M) communication, and often acts as a protocol translator \cite[p. 3]{DevelopMuna}. In this methodology, the gateway is the central System Under Test. The normative security mechanisms (e.g., authentication, protocol filtering) are explicitly evaluated at this node.
	
	\item \textbf{Test Execution and Measurement Zone:} This zone contains the attacker machine and analysis tools. A dedicated workstation runs specialized penetration testing frameworks, such as Kali Linux or Metasploit. Furthermore, this zone acts as a measurement enclave. It captures network traffic via port mirroring, retains syslog messages, and analyzes statistical performance metrics \cite[p. 3]{8f3698f787af48ccb0aa063527bbb4de} \Cite[p. 5]{DevelopMuna}.
	
	\item \textbf{Local Networking Backbone:} A dedicated physical switch and cabling infrastructure connects the aforementioned zones. This backbone manages the controlled flow of test traffic. It enables researchers to manipulate traffic intentionally, introduce packet flight time uncertainty (delay and jitter), and simulate real-world channel degradation \cite[p. 3]{8f3698f787af48ccb0aa063527bbb4de}.
\end{enumerate}

These components form a layer-based, modular platform. A modular structure ensures that the testbed remains adaptable for various industrial devices \cite[p. 4]{siboni2016securitytestbedinternetthings}. The testbed architecture must fulfill strict functional requirements. It must support targeted tests against known vulnerabilities, such as injection flaws, broken authentication, and security misconfigurations \cite[p. 3]{siboni2016securitytestbedinternetthings}. Furthermore, the architecture must facilitate the structured execution of threat scenarios based on the STRIDE model \cite[p. 9]{DevelopMuna}.

\section{Test Execution Framework and Governance}
\label{sec:execution_governance}

Once the test environment is established and the test cases are specified, the actual execution phase begins. This phase defines how the tests are practically conducted, how the results are legally documented, and who bears the organizational responsibility for the outcomes.

\subsection{Test Execution and Tooling}
\label{subsec:execution_tooling}
%TODO Quellen
To systematically verify the targeted security functions, the execution phase relies on specialized, industry-standard auditing tools. A centralized security distribution, such as Kali Linux, typically serves as the methodological foundation for the execution node. Instead of writing custom exploit scripts, the methodology utilizes established tool categories depending on the specific test case and asset:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Network Scanners:} Tools like Nmap are used to verify open ports, running services, and the correct configuration of physical network interfaces.
	\item \textbf{Interception Proxies:} Tools such as OWASP ZAP or Burp Suite are deployed to intercept, inspect, and manipulate HTTP/API requests to test authentication and authorization mechanisms.
	\item \textbf{Stress Testing Utilities:} Tools like Siege or Slowloris are utilized to evaluate system availability and resilience against Denial-of-Service (DoS) conditions.
	\item \textbf{Brute-Force Applications:} Tools like Hydra test the robustness of login portals against automated credential-guessing attacks.
\end{itemize}

The execution strictly follows the step-by-step procedures defined in the standardized test data sheets (see Section \ref{subsec:test_specification}).

\subsection{Evidence Collection and Documentation}
\label{subsec:evidence_collection}
%TODO Quelle
Executing a test is insufficient if the outcome cannot be formally proven. Article 10(2) and Annex IV Part A of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 explicitly mandate comprehensive technical documentation to demonstrate conformity \cite{EU2023_1230}. Therefore, meticulous evidence collection is a mandatory methodological step.

For every executed test case, digital artifacts must be recorded. This includes raw console outputs (logs), network traffic captures (PCAP files), and screenshots showing blocked access attempts or error codes (e.g., HTTP 403 Forbidden). These artifacts serve as the indisputable empirical proof that a security mechanism successfully mitigated an attack. In the context of regulatory compliance, this evidence acts as the mandatory certification proof (colloquially the "TÜV-Beweis"). Without this attached evidence, a test case cannot legitimately receive a passing verdict, regardless of the observed system behavior.

\section{Evaluation Metrics and Compliance Assessment}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}
%TODO Quelle
To objectively assess the system's security posture and the framework's methodological quality, quantitative evaluation criteria are required. This assessment occurs at two levels: the discrete verdicts for individual test cases and the aggregated statistical metrics that evaluate overall engineering rigor and regulatory compliance.

\subsection{Test Case and Requirement Verdicts}
\label{subsec:verdicts}

At the level of the individual test case, a verdict is assigned based on the defined acceptance criteria and the sufficiency of the collected evidence. Four discrete verdicts are distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{PASS:} The test case was executed, all mandatory acceptance criteria are satisfied, and the collected evidence is sufficient to prove successful mitigation.
	\item \textbf{PARTIAL:} The test case was executed and mandatory criteria are satisfied, but non-critical criteria remain unfulfilled or the defense mechanism showed minor degradation.
	\item \textbf{FAIL:} The test case was executed, and a mandatory security control was bypassed, highlighting an exploitable vulnerability.
	\item \textbf{NOT APPLICABLE (N/A):} An applicability decision taken prior to execution. It requires a documented technical justification based on system architecture or the defined scope limits.
\end{itemize}

These test case verdicts are subsequently aggregated to evaluate the fulfillment of the overarching legal requirement. A requirement is verdicted \textit{FAIL} if any associated test case fails. It achieves a \textit{PASS} only if every associated test case passes. 

\subsection{Core Framework Metrics}
\label{subsec:framework_metrics}
%TODO Quelle
To evaluate both the technical security level of the industrial gateway and the quality of the testing methodology itself, four core Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) are defined. All metrics are calculated excluding logically justified \textit{N/A} elements to prevent data skewing.

\textbf{Requirement Compliance Rate ($RCR$)} is the primary legal indicator. It measures the strict fulfillment of the derived normative obligations from Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 and IEC 62443-4-2.
\begin{equation}
	RCR = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements verdicted PASS}}{\text{Total applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Threat Mitigation Coverage ($TMC$)} evaluates the technical security posture from an engineering perspective. Referencing the STRIDE threat model defined in Section \ref{subsec:stride}, this metric calculates the proportion of identified, asset-specific threat vectors that were successfully blocked by the tested security functions.
\begin{equation}
	TMC = \frac{\text{Successfully mitigated STRIDE threats}}{\text{Total modeled STRIDE threats for tested assets}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Test Effectiveness Ratio ($TER$)} measures the methodological quality of the test design. A test is considered highly effective if it yields a definitive empirical outcome (\textit{PASS} or \textit{FAIL}) rather than failing due to testbed errors, tool crashes, or inconclusive system behavior.
\begin{equation}
	TER = \frac{\text{Executed test cases yielding a definitive PASS or FAIL}}{\text{Total executed test cases}}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Evidence Traceability Score ($ETS$)} assesses audit readiness. It indicates the proportion of executed tests supported by complete, traceable digital artifacts (e.g., PCAP files, system logs). This directly addresses the strict evidentiary obligations for the technical documentation under Article 10(2) of the Machinery Regulation.
\begin{equation}
	ETS = \frac{\text{Executed test cases with comprehensive digital evidence}}{\text{Total executed test cases}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{subsec:methodology_limitations}
%TODO Quelle
While this functional security testing methodology ensures systemic traceability and verifies the correct operation of planned security mechanisms, its inherent limitations must be acknowledged. 

First, functional security testing does not guarantee absolute immunity against novel zero-day exploits. It confirms that a mechanism (e.g., authentication) works as intended under known attack vectors, but it does not aggressively hunt for deep architectural vulnerabilities, such as memory corruption bugs, which would require dedicated dynamic analysis or fuzzing. 

Second, the effectiveness of this framework relies heavily on the quality of the initial threat modeling and asset mapping (see Section \ref{subsec:derivation_mapping}). If a critical asset or threat vector is misidentified or omitted during the derivation phase, the resulting test cases will inherently contain blind spots. Consequently, this methodology should be viewed as a foundational pillar for regulatory compliance verification, complementing rather than replacing exhaustive penetration testing.
