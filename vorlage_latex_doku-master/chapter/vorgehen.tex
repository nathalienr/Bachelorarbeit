\chapter{Methodology} 
\label{chap:methodology} 

This chapter describes the methodological approach used to develop and evaluate the proposed security testing framework. Building upon the theoretical foundations presented in Chapter \ref{chap:fundamentals}, the research follows the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) proposed by \citeauthor{Peffers} DSRM provides a structured process for addressing practical problems through the design, development, demonstration, and evaluation of artifacts \cite[p. 46]{Peffers}. The objective of this research is the development of a standards-based functional security testing framework for industrial gateways. The framework is intended to enable the traceable, repeatable, and systematic verification of security functions derived from the requirements of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230, prEN 50742, and the IEC 62443 series. Within the scope of this thesis, the activities of the DSRM process are mapped to the corresponding chapters. Problem identification and objective definition are presented in Chapter \ref{chap:introduction}. The design and development phase is addressed in this chapter through the derivation of security requirements and the development of the proposed testing framework. The demonstration and evaluation phases are conducted in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}, where the framework is applied to an industrial gateway as a case study. The communication phase is represented by the documentation, discussion, and dissemination of the research results through this thesis.

\section{Derivation of Security Requirements}
\label{sec:security_requirements}


The aim is to produce a structured, traceable set of product requirements for an industrial gateway at the IT/OT interface, grounded in the legal obligations of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230. The complete requirement catalogue, prEN 50742 mapping, and IEC 62443-4-2 mapping are documented in Appendix \ref{app:requirement_derivation}.


The derivation follows a three-stage hierarchical chain: 

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \hyperref[subsec:eu_machinery_regulation]{\textbf{Regulation (EU) 2023/1230}} defines legally binding protection objectives for machinery, including explicit cybersecurity obligations.
	\item \hyperref[subsec:pren_50742]{\textbf{prEN 50742}} operationalizes these objectives for connected machinery and links them to the IEC 62443 series.
	\item \hyperref[subsec:iec_62443]{\textbf{IEC 62443-4-2}} specifies component-level technical security requirements (CRs, REs, EDRs) that can be verified by functional testing.
\end{enumerate}

None of the three sources is independently sufficient. Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 defines mandatory objectives but not component-level technical specifications. IEC 62443-4-2 provides precise technical controls but does not by itself establish which are required for Machinery Regulation conformity. PrEN 50742 preserves legal traceability while introducing technically interpretable obligations that can be mapped to IEC 62443-4-2.


This derivation does not combine the two conformity assessment paths defined by prEN 50742 (Approach A and Approach B, Section \ref{subsec:pren_50742}). Requirement extraction draws exclusively on Sections 4, 5, and 7 of prEN 50742, since these sections contain the clause-level normative technical content applicable to machinery products irrespective of the conformity path a manufacturer ultimately selects. Sections 6 and 8, which are specific to Approach B, are drawn upon only where they explicitly name a target standard or clause (Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_pren50742}). The subsequent mapping of these clauses onto IEC 62443-4-2 Component Requirements is therefore not an adoption of Approach B as the conformity route. It is performed solely to establish technical traceability and verification coverage for the derived requirements, independent of the conformity path a given manufacturer may choose.

\hyperref[subsec:eu_machinery_regulation]{\textbf{Regulation (EU) 2023/1230}} establishes that machinery certified under a cybersecurity certification scheme pursuant to Regulation (EU) 2019/881 is presumed to comply with the essential health and safety requirements of Annex III insofar as these requirements are covered by the certification. The cybersecurity provisions explicitly addressed by this presumption are:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Section 1.1.9} (Protection against corruption): obligations concerning safety-relevant hardware interfaces, software and data protection, and evidence collection.
	\item \textbf{Section 1.2.1} (Safety and reliability of control systems): obligations concerning resilience against malicious third-party attempts and logging of safety-relevant interventions.
\end{itemize}

Each regulatory clause was decomposed analytically into candidate requirements. The decomposition applied the following four criteria:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Traceability:} The requirement must trace directly to an explicit obligation in \hyperref[subsec:eu_machinery_regulation]{\textbf{Regulation (EU) 2023/1230}}.
	\item \textbf{Atomicity:} Each requirement must express exactly one verifiable technical property. Clauses combining multiple independent obligations were split into separate requirements.
	\item \textbf{Verifiability:} The requirement must be testable in principle by a functional test or a clearly delimited technical assessment.
	\item \textbf{Product relevance:} The requirement must describe a property of the gateway as a product, not an organizational, documentation, or purely functional-safety obligation.
\end{enumerate}

Each candidate requirement was compared with \hyperref[subsec:pren_50742]{\textbf{prEN 50742}} to determine whether the draft standard provides a technically defensible interpretation for the machinery domain. Four mapping types were defined: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Direct}: clause-level match
	\item \textbf{Partial}: scope or trigger condition differs 
	\item \textbf{Indirect}: related principle, no clause-level match
	\item \textbf{Not identified}: confirmed gap or conflict
\end{itemize}

The Indirect category is retained for methodological completeness, since a related-principle match without a clause-level counterpart is a possible outcome of the mapping procedure. No requirement in the final dataset was classified into this category.

Where \hyperref[subsec:pren_50742]{\textbf{prEN 50742}} provided clause-level support, the corresponding IEC 62443-4-2 CRs, REs, and EDRs were identified. One additional mapping type was introduced for IEC 62443-4-2: \textbf{No direct mapping} (no CR addresses the obligation).

Controls were selected only where the connection to the regulatory source could be substantiated through the derivation chain. The role of the Foundational Requirements (see Section \ref{subsec:iec_62443}) was classificatory. The selection of specific CRs was driven by the protection goals established in the earlier stages.

Confirmed normative gaps arise for two requirements within the scope of Sections 1.1.9 and 1.2.1. RQ-010 addresses resilience against malicious third-party attempts at a system level. IEC 62443-4-2 assigns such process-level obligations to IEC 62443-4-1, and no component-level CR provides a direct mapping. RQ-013 presents a confirmed conflict: the MVO requires logging of uploaded safety software versions, whereas prEN 50742 explicitly excludes binary software versions from the tracing log. These two gaps define the positions where the standard-based test approach must be supplemented by product-specific verification.

The detailed mapping tables are provided in Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_pren50742} (prEN 50742) and Appendix \ref{app:sec:mapping_iec62443} (IEC 62443-4-2).

CR and RE selection under IEC62443-4-2 depends on the targeted Component Security Level Capability (SL-C), since Requirement Enhancements incrementally strengthen a base CR at higher target levels (Section\ref{subsec:iec_62443}). Unless stated otherwise, the derivation assumes a target level of SL-C2, consistent with the upper end of the range typically applied to machinery components under prEN50742 ApproachB (Section\ref{subsec:pren_50742}), and this target level is applied consistently across all CR/RE selections in Appendix\ref{app:sec:mapping_iec62443}.

\section{Functional Security Testing Framework Architecture}
\label{sec:framework_architecture}

\subsection{Scope and Boundaries}
\label{subsec:scope_boundaries}


This thesis is delimited to \textbf{Functional verification}, i.e., the testing of implemented security functions against the requirement baseline (Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}), as opposed to \textbf{robustness testing} (fuzzing, penetration testing) at the gateway's IT/OT interfaces, as already distinguished in Section \ref{sec:security_testing_approaches} and Section \ref{subsec:vulnerabilities_fuzzing_pentesting}.

Within this scope, the framework verifies that the security functions and countermeasures mandated by RQ-001 to RQ-014 are actually implemented and operate as specified at the interfaces identified as gateway assets in Section \ref{subsec:attack_surface_asset_identification}. Fuzzing, penetration testing, and open-ended vulnerability scanning are explicitly out of scope, consistent with the exclusions already defined in Chapter \ref{chap:introduction}. These techniques search for unknown weaknesses rather than verifying a specified, requirement-bound behavior, and therefore answer a different question than the one posed by this framework \cite[p. 8, pp. 29]{richier2011security}.


\subsection{Traceability Model}
\label{subsec:traceability_model}
The traceability chain reflects the perspective of the tester executing the framework, linking an abstract regulatory obligation to a concrete, evidenced test result. Each relationship in the chain is one-to-many. One requirement may apply to several assets, and one test category may be instantiated by several test cases.

Requirement $\rightarrow$ Asset $\rightarrow$ Threat $\rightarrow$ Test Category $\rightarrow$ Test Method $\rightarrow$ Test Case $\rightarrow$ Evidence and Verdict

The chain is read as follows. 
The \textbf{MVO/IEC Requirement} is one of the RQ-001 to RQ-014 requirements together with its mapped IEC 62443-4-2 CR/RE/EDR (Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}).
 
The \textbf{Asset} is the concrete gateway asset (Section \ref{subsec:test_object}) to which the requirement applies, anchoring the abstract obligation to a testable component of the test object. 

The \textbf{STRIDE Threat} is the threat category (Section \ref{subsec:stride}) that the requirement is intended to counter at that asset. 

The \textbf{Test Category} groups requirement-asset-threat combinations by verification logic, as defined in Section \ref{subsec:test_case_taxonomy}. 

The \textbf{Test Method} is the established functional testing technique assigned to that category (Section \ref{sec:security_testing_approaches}), so that the tester verifies the correct operation of a planned security mechanism rather than searching for unknown flaws \cite[p. 82]{1341418}. 

The \textbf{Test Case} is the concrete instantiation of that method, applied with a specific tool and procedure to the test object (Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}). 

Finally, \textbf{Evidence and Verdict} comprises the recorded artifacts (logs, captures, screenshots) and the discrete verdict assigned to the test case (Section \ref{subsec:verdict_scheme}), in line with the documentation and reporting discipline required of structured security testing \cite[p. 7-3, p. 7-5]{nist_sp800_115}.

For the design phase, an analogous forward traceability from threat to control is already established by the TARA workflow (Section \ref{subsec:tara}), which proceeds from threat and impact analysis to a documented risk treatment decision \cite[pp. 46]{doCarmo2025}. 
The chain defined here starts from the mitigation already established through the TARA process and the requirement derivation described in Section \ref{sec:security_requirements} (see Section \ref{subsec:risk_treatment_strategies}). It then carries this mitigation forward, from the tester's perspective, through the concrete asset, threat, and test method to an executed and evidenced verdict. Consequently, no mitigation is credited as effective solely on the basis of its specification.

Every test case therefore carries an unbroken reference back to its originating regulatory clause. This traceability chain is the structural precondition for the coverage metric introduced in Section \ref{subsec:coverage_metric} and for the technical documentation obligation under Article 10(2) and Annex IV Part A of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 \cite{EU2023_1230}.


\subsection{Functional Security Test Classification}
\label{subsec:test_case_taxonomy}

This subsection operationalizes the consolidated requirement baseline (Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}) and the abstract \textbf{Test Category} node of the traceability chain (Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model}) into a reusable classification for functional security test cases. It is a testing-oriented structure whose purpose is to organize the design, execution, and evidence collection of the test cases developed in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}.

A direct, one-to-one classification along the seven IEC 62443 Foundational Requirements (Section \ref{subsec:iec_62443}) was considered but rejected as the primary organizing principle. Table \ref{tab:req_categories} already shows that the derived requirement categories do not align one-to-one with individual FRs. An FR-partitioned taxonomy would therefore either split a single operational security function across several domains or duplicate identical test procedures under multiple headings. 
Instead, the six requirement categories established in Table \ref{tab:req_categories} are adopted as the primary test domains, since they already reflect an operational grouping of the requirement baseline by shared verification logic. The IEC 62443 Foundational Requirements are retained as a normative cross-reference layer attached to each domain, not as the classification axis itself. This preserves normative traceability without forcing an artificial partition of the requirement baseline. Table \ref{tab:test_domains} summarizes the resulting six test domains together with their normative FR cross-references and representative functional subdomains.

\subsection{Verification Lifecycle and Governance}
\label{subsec:verification_lifecycle_governance}



Verification activities defined in Sections \ref{subsec:traceability_model} and \ref{subsec:test_case_taxonomy} are not executed as an isolated activity. They must be anchored to defined stages of the product lifecycle so that a test case is generated, executed, and disposed of at a determinable point in the development process. Secure Software Development Life Cycle (SSDLC) models embed security-related activities, including verification and testing, into every phase of an otherwise conventional development process rather than confining them to a single stage prior to release \cite{ibm_ssdlc_2025} \cite{geeksforgeeks_ssdlc_2026}. Commonly cited phase models separate requirements, analysis, planning, design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance \cite{ibm_ssdlc_2025}. DevSecOps lifecycle models describe a comparable seven-stage loop of Plan, Code, Build, Test, Release, Operate, and Monitor and feedback, in which findings observed at runtime are fed back into planning rather than remaining isolated \cite{cloudaware_devsecops_lifecycle_2026}.

Consistent with the scope restriction defined in Section \ref{subsec:scope_boundaries}, this thesis addresses only the verification-relevant subset of such a lifecycle. Upstream requirement, design, and implementation stages, and downstream deployment and monitoring stages, are treated as boundary conditions that supply inputs to, or consume outputs from, the verification stages defined here.

The generic test case data sheet already assigns each of its fields to a Design, Preparation, Execution, or Evidence/Evaluation phase (Table \ref{tab:test_case_sheet}). These four phases embed the traceability chain of Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model} into the product lifecycle. Design-phase activities are carried out once the requirement baseline (Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}) and the asset and threat assignment (Section \ref{subsec:test_case_taxonomy}) are stable. Preparation-phase activities configure the test bed against the test object description of Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}. Execution-phase activities take place once the corresponding implementation is complete. Evidence and Evaluation-phase activities close out the requirement baseline before a release decision is taken, consuming the verdict and coverage scheme defined in Section \ref{sec:evaluation_metrics}.


Responsibility for these four phases is assigned through a Responsible-Accountable-Consulted-Informed (RACI) matrix, an established responsibility-assignment method in which exactly one role is Accountable for a given activity while one or more roles are Responsible, Consulted, or Informed \cite{cybersierra_security_raci_2026}\cite{su13041806}. This thesis adapts the RACI method as a governance instrument for the verification lifecycle to make explicit which role executes, authorises, or is merely informed of a given verification activity. Six generic roles are distinguished for this purpose \Cite[p. 6]{Schmidt2026CybersecurityTestProcess}: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item Security Engineering: which holds technical accountability for test scope and execution integrity
	\item System/Requirements Engineering: consulted where a requirement or asset boundary is affected
	\item Test Engineering: responsible for procedure preparation, execution, and verdict assignment
	\item Software/Firmware Development: consulted on findings and tasked with implementing corrective actions
	\item Quality/Compliance Management: responsible for coverage review and evidence governance
	\item Release Authority: accountable for the release decision.
\end{itemize}

Distinguishing the role responsible for executing a verification activity from the role accountable for closing it out reflects the practical division of labour documented in secure development lifecycle literature, where the assignment of security tasks among developers and testers shapes how such tasks are executed in practice \cite[p. 5]{219394}.

IEC 62443-4-1 associates systematic security testing prior to release with Practice 5, Security Verification and Validation Testing (SV), already introduced in Section \ref{subsec:iec_62443} \cite[p. 37]{DIN_EN_IEC_62443_4_1_2018}. The lifecycle phases and the role assignments defined above are aligned with Practice 5 thematically.

Design and Preparation activities correspond to the planning and specification of security test cases prior to execution. Execution activities correspond to the execution of security tests against specified security requirements, consistent with SVV-1 (Security requirements testing), which requires a process demonstrating that the product's security functions meet the security requirements and correctly handle error scenarios and invalid inputs \cite[p. 38]{DIN_EN_IEC_62443_4_1_2018}. Evidence and Evaluation activities correspond to the documentation and disposition of test results required by Practice 5 \cite[p. 37]{DIN_EN_IEC_62443_4_1_2018}. 


\section{Evaluation Metrics and Assessment Scheme}
\label{sec:evaluation_metrics}

Evaluating the proposed functional security testing framework requires criteria at more than one level of aggregation. This thesis distinguishes three evaluation levels: 
\begin{itemize}
	\item the result of an individual test case
	\item the fulfilment of a single security requirement derived in Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}
	\item the quality of the testing methodology as a whole.
\end{itemize}
This distinction follows from the traceability chain of Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model}, in which a single requirement traces to one or more test cases, and the set of all requirement-level outcomes in turn characterises the methodology applied to the test object in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}. 

Test metrics literature similarly separates metrics addressing individual test outcomes from metrics addressing the test process itself, including its maturity and effectiveness \cite[pp. 144, p. 217]{Witte2019}. This section defines the evaluation criteria, decision rules, and metrics for all three levels before they are applied in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation}.

At the level of the individual \textbf{test case}, a verdict is assigned using the acceptance criteria, the classification of each acceptance criterion as mandatory or non-critical, and the sufficiency of the collected evidence, all recorded in the test case data sheet (Table \ref{tab:test_case_sheet}). Based on acceptance-oriented assessment concepts reported in the literature and adapted to the evaluation methodology developed in this thesis, four verdicts are distinguished \cite[p. 1553]{11050621} \cite[p. 23]{richier2011security}. PASS is assigned when the test case has been executed, all mandatory acceptance criteria are satisfied, and the evidence collected is sufficient to support this conclusion. PARTIAL is assigned when the test case has been executed, all mandatory acceptance criteria are satisfied, and only non-critical acceptance criteria remain unfulfilled or are only partly demonstrated. FAIL is assigned when the test case has been executed and a mandatory or critical acceptance criterion is violated. NOT APPLICABLE is an applicability decision taken before execution rather than a test result. It requires a documented technical justification based on the intended use, system architecture, available interfaces, or the scope defined in Section \ref{subsec:scope_boundaries}. Insufficient evidence never results in a PASS verdict, and a documented technical justification is required for every NOT APPLICABLE decision.

At the level of the individual \textbf{security requirement}, the goal is to decide whether the requirement is actually covered by the testing framework, that is, whether the applied test methods verify the corresponding obligation of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230. Since a requirement typically traces to more than one test case (Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model}), the underlying test-case verdicts are aggregated into a single requirement-level verdict, using the same four values. A requirement is verdicted FAIL if any test case tracing to it is verdicted FAIL, or if a critical acceptance criterion is violated. A requirement is verdicted PASS only if every test case tracing to it is verdicted PASS. A requirement is verdicted PARTIAL if none of its test cases is verdicted FAIL, but at least one is verdicted PARTIAL. A requirement is verdicted NOT APPLICABLE where its non-applicability has been documented and justified (Section \ref{subsec:scope_boundaries}), independent of any test case verdict.
Three regulatory roles are distinguished in this framework. Security requirements define what must be verified. They are derived from Annex III, Sections 1.1.9 and 1.2.1 of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 (Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}). Evaluation metrics, defined in this section, define how the verification of these requirements is assessed. Technical documentation requirements define what evidence must be retained. A metric defined in this section assesses verification activities. It does not itself constitute a security requirement or a documentation requirement.

At the level of the \textbf{testing methodology}, the question is no longer the verdict of a single requirement, but how much of the entire requirement baseline the applied test methods actually cover. The requirement baseline comprises the fourteen requirements RQ-001 to RQ-014 derived in Section \ref{sec:security_requirements}. A requirement counts as applicable unless it carries a documented NOT APPLICABLE justification. 
Every metric below is computed only over applicable requirements, so that a documented exclusion is never counted as a coverage gap. Four separate quantities are tracked, since a requirement can be specified without being executed, executed without being fulfilled, and fulfilled without complete supporting evidence. How many applicable requirements have a specified test case, how many of these have actually been executed, how many are verdicted PASS, and how many are documented with complete evidence. Following the simple, ratio-based style used for test-process metrics in the literature \cite[pp. 145]{Witte2019}, each quantity below is expressed as a single count divided by a reference count.

Requirement Mapping Coverage is defined as
\begin{equation}
	C_{map} = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements with a specified test case}}{\text{Applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}
the proportion of applicable requirements for which at least one suitable test case has been specified. The existence of a test case specification is not treated as evidence that the requirement has actually been tested (based on \cite[p. 223]{Witte2019}).

Executed Requirement Coverage is defined as
\begin{equation}
	C_{exec} = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements with executed test cases}}{\text{Applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}
the proportion of applicable requirements for which the specified test cases have actually been executed (based on \cite[p. 223]{Witte2019}).

Requirement Fulfilment Rate is defined as
\begin{equation}
	C_{fulfil} = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements verdicted PASS}}{\text{Applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}
the proportion of applicable requirements assessed as PASS (based on \cite[p. 223]{Witte2019}). The counts of requirements verdicted PARTIAL and FAIL are reported separately alongside $C_{fulfil}$. Coverage and fulfilment are not combined into a single value, since a fully executed requirement baseline may still contain unfulfilled requirements.

Forward Traceability describes the extent to which an applicable requirement can be traced through the traceability chain of Section \ref{subsec:traceability_model} to its test case, evidence, and verdict:
\begin{equation}
	T_{fwd} = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements traceable to a test case, evidence, and a recorded verdict}}{\text{Applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}

Backward Traceability describes the extent to which each executed test case can be traced back to a regulatory, normative, security, threat, risk, or mitigation source:
\begin{equation}
	T_{bwd} = \frac{\text{Executed test cases traceable to a regulatory, normative, security, threat, risk, or mitigation source}}{\text{Executed test cases}}
\end{equation}
Backward Traceability is intended to identify tool-based test cases included without a justified requirement or risk relationship.

Evidence Completeness describes the completeness of the evidence package generated for an executed test case, relative to the fields already defined in the test case data sheet (Table \ref{tab:test_case_sheet}) together with the identification of the system under test, its hardware and firmware version, the test environment and tool version, the observed result, and, for failed test cases, a defect or deviation reference  \cite[p. 219]{Witte2019} \cite[p. 147]{Witte2019}:
\begin{equation}
	EC = \frac{\text{Executed test cases with a complete evidence package}}{\text{Executed test cases}}
\end{equation}

Technical Documentation Evidence Coverage is defined as
\begin{equation}
	TDEC = \frac{\text{Applicable requirements with complete, traceable evidence suitable for technical documentation}}{\text{Applicable requirements}}
\end{equation}
$TDEC$ indicates the proportion of applicable requirements for which the evidence required under Article 10(2) and Annex IV Part A of Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 has been generated \cite{EU2023_1230}. It does not by itself prove complete conformity or certification, and this thesis does not claim otherwise.

Repeatability is defined as
\begin{equation}
	R = \frac{\text{Repeated executions with a verdict identical to the first execution}}{\text{Total repeated executions}}
\end{equation}
Equivalent conditions require the gateway configuration, firmware version, test-tool version, test parameters, network setup, and reset state to remain controlled between executions. Because repeated executions in Chapter \ref{chap:implementation_results_evaluation} are performed in the same laboratory and by the same tester, $R$ reports repeatability under these conditions only; it does not establish reproducibility across laboratories or testers.

Practical Applicability is assessed using measurable indicators rather than a single composite score, since an unsubstantiated overall usability rating would not be operationally defined. The indicators recorded per test case include setup time, execution time, analysis time, documentation time, the number of manual steps, the level of expertise required, the effort needed to restore the test object to its precondition state, and the degree of automation achieved 
\cite[p. 145]{Witte2019} \cite[p. 228]{Witte2019}.

Mitigation Verification Capability is defined as
\begin{equation}
	MVC = \frac{\text{Evaluated security controls with a conclusive, evidence-based verdict}}{\text{Total evaluated security controls}}
\end{equation}
$MVC$ indicates the proportion of the evaluated security controls for which the methodology produces a conclusive, evidence-based verdict on the behaviour of the control under the defined test conditions. The methodology verifies this behaviour. It does not by itself reduce the underlying risk assessed in the TARA (Section \ref{subsec:tara}).
